The mitochondrion is often called the powerhouse of the cell because it converts nutrients into adenosine triphosphate, the molecule cells use as their main energy currency. Nearly every eukaryotic cell contains hundreds to thousands of these organelles, and their number scales with how much energy a particular cell type needs.
Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. Plants, algae, and some bacteria capture photons with chlorophyll, split water molecules to release oxygen, and use the freed electrons to build sugar from carbon dioxide. This single process is responsible for nearly all the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere.
Water is unusual among common substances because its solid form, ice, is less dense than its liquid form. This happens because water molecules form a hydrogen-bonded lattice when frozen that holds them slightly farther apart than in the liquid state. As a result, ice floats, insulating the water below it and letting aquatic life survive through winter.
The speed of light in a vacuum is approximately 299,792 kilometers per second, and nothing with mass can reach or exceed it. This isn't just a practical limit — it's built into the structure of spacetime itself, as described by special relativity, and it links space and time into a single four-dimensional fabric.
DNA carries genetic information using four chemical bases: adenine, thymine, guanine, and cytosine. These bases pair up in a predictable way, adenine with thymine and guanine with cytosine, which is what lets DNA be copied accurately every time a cell divides.
An element's position on the periodic table reflects the number of protons in its nucleus, called the atomic number. Elements in the same column, or group, tend to share chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons in their outermost shell.
Earthquakes happen when stress built up along a fault line is suddenly released, sending seismic waves through the ground. The point underground where the rupture starts is called the focus, and the point on the surface directly above it is the epicenter.
A black hole forms when enough mass collapses into a small enough space that its gravity prevents even light from escaping. The boundary beyond which nothing can return is called the event horizon, and it isn't a physical surface but a point of no return defined purely by gravity.
The human immune system has two main branches: the innate response, which reacts quickly but generically to any threat, and the adaptive response, which is slower to activate but produces antibodies tailored to a specific pathogen and can remember it for years afterward.
Plate tectonics describes how Earth's outer shell is broken into large rigid plates that slowly move atop the softer mantle beneath them. Where plates collide, spread apart, or slide past each other, they produce mountains, ocean ridges, and earthquake zones respectively.
Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions inside living cells without being consumed by the reaction. Each enzyme has a specific shape that fits its target molecule, much like a lock and key, which is why a small change in that shape can stop an enzyme from working at all.
The greenhouse effect occurs when gases like carbon dioxide and methane trap heat that would otherwise radiate from Earth's surface back into space. This effect is entirely natural and necessary for a habitable climate; the concern with human emissions is the added intensity, not the mechanism itself.
Neurons communicate by sending electrical impulses down a long fiber called an axon, then releasing chemical messengers called neurotransmitters across a tiny gap called a synapse to reach the next neuron. A single neuron can form thousands of these synaptic connections.
The Doppler effect explains why an ambulance siren sounds higher in pitch as it approaches and lower as it moves away. Sound waves bunch together in front of a moving source and stretch out behind it, changing the frequency an observer perceives without the source itself changing at all.
Vaccines work by training the immune system to recognize a pathogen without causing the disease itself, typically using a weakened, inactivated, or partial version of the pathogen, or in newer designs, genetic instructions that prompt cells to build a harmless piece of it.
