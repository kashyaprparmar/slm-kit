The lighthouse keeper woke before dawn, as he had every morning for thirty years.
Wind pressed against the tower, and the sea below churned grey and patient.
He climbed the spiral stair, counting the steps out of habit rather than need.
At the top, the great lamp had burned through the night without complaint.
He trimmed the wick, polished the brass, and watched the horizon turn from black to pearl.

Far out, a fishing boat rocked between the swells. The keeper raised a hand,
though no one could have seen him at that distance. It was enough, he thought,
to keep the light. To be the small steady thing that others steered by.

In the village, the baker opened her shutters and let the smell of warm bread
spill into the lane. Children ran past with satchels bouncing, late as always.
The clock in the square kept its slow and certain time, and the day began.

A traveller arrived on the noon coach, carrying a single leather case.
He asked for a room with a view of the water, and the innkeeper obliged.
That evening he sat by the window and wrote letters he would never send,
watching the lighthouse blink its patient message across the darkening bay.

Autumn came, and then winter. The sea grew hard and the nights grew long.
The keeper kept the light. The baker kept the ovens warm. The clock kept time.
And the small town went on, as small towns do, held together by habit and by care,
by the ordinary courage of people who rise in the dark to do their quiet work.

Spring returned in its own time, unhurried and complete.
Ice loosened from the rocks and the gulls came wheeling back.
The traveller left on the morning coach, his case a little heavier with pages.
He did not say goodbye, but he looked up once at the tower and nodded,
as one keeper of small lights might nod to another across a great distance.

Years later, people would still tell the story, though the details changed with each telling.
Some said the keeper had never existed, that the lamp had burned on its own.
Others swore they had shaken his hand. It did not matter greatly which was true.
What mattered was the light, and that it had been kept, and that ships came safely home.
